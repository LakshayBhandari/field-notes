Password hashing & the bcrypt cost factor

bcrypt.hashSync(password, 10) (backend/index.js L62) does three things: generates a random salt, runs a deliberately slow key-derivation 2ⁿ times, and returns a single string that embeds the algorithm, the cost, the salt, AND the hash — all in one. Login uses bcrypt.compareSync(password, user.password) (L89), which extracts the embedded salt/cost, re-derives the hash with them, and compares.

Anatomy of a stored hash

Open db.json and look at a real password field. It is not one opaque blob — it is four pieces glued by $:

$2b$10$N9qo8uLOickgx2ZMRZoMye  IjZAgcfl7p92ldGxad68LJZdL17lhWy
└┬┘ └┬┘ └──────────┬─────────┘  └────────────┬────────────┘
 │   │             │                         │
 │   │       SALT (22 chars,            HASH (depends on
 │   │       random per signup,         password + salt + cost)
 │   │       PUBLIC — stored
 │   │       in plain sight)
 │   │
 │  COST: 2ⁿ iterations (here 10 → 1024 rounds)
 │
 ALGO: 2b = bcrypt

Three of those four pieces (algo, cost, salt) are not secret. Anyone with the database also has them. Only the password itself is unknown — and the whole point of the design is that re-deriving it is expensive.

The cost factor and the 2ⁿ curve

The 10 is the cost factor. Iterations = 2ⁿ. Each +1 doubles the work.

cost   iterations       relative   ≈ time per hash
────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  8       256              1×          ~12 ms
 10     1 024              4×          ~50 ms   ← your code
 12     4 096             16×         ~200 ms
 14    16 384             64×         ~800 ms
 16    65 536            256×         ~3.2 s

Cost 10 → 14 is +4, which is 2⁴ = 16× more work, not "a bit more." It's not vague complexity — it's a quantifiable knob you turn as hardware gets faster.

The frequency asymmetry — the actual point of slowness

Raising cost slows the server and the attacker by the same multiplier. So why is it a win? Because the two sides run very different counts of hashes:

SAME 16× per-hash slowdown applied to:

  Your server (once per /login request)
  ████ × 1
  → 16× of "once" ≈ unnoticeable

  Attacker brute-forcing a leaked DB (once per guess)
  ████ × 1 000 000 000 …
  → 16× of "a billion" = the difference between
    cracking in 1 week vs 16 weeks

That is the whole game: same multiplier, wildly different impact, because of how many times each side runs it.

When does this slowness actually matter?

For a normal login it doesn't. 50 ms is invisible. Slowness only earns its keep after something bad has already happened:

NORMAL DAY
  user → POST /login → bcrypt.compare (≈50 ms) → ok
  No attacker present. bcrypt could be 1 ms or 50 ms.
  Doesn't matter. No defence needed.

AFTER A DB BREACH (db.json is stolen)
  attacker, OFFLINE, on their own machines:
    for each guess in [billions]:
      bcrypt(guess, leaked_salt, cost) == leaked_hash?
  ↑ THIS is the only moment bcrypt's slowness pays off.
    It caps the attacker at ~thousands/sec instead of
    ~billions/sec.

How compareSync works without decrypting

Bcrypt is one-way — there is no decryption. To verify a login:

compareSync(inputPassword, storedHash):
  1. Read algo, cost, salt out of storedHash
     (they are right there in the prefix — see anatomy above)
  2. Re-run the SAME computation:
        bcrypt(inputPassword, salt, cost)
  3. Compare the new result against storedHash byte-for-byte
     → equal  → password correct
     → not    → wrong password

The verification works because the salt and cost are public — they have to be reused at compare-time. There is nothing to "decrypt."

⚠️ IMPORTANT

Common misread: "Higher cost just makes the hash 'more complex.'"

Actually: the output string is the same length whether cost is 10 or 14. What changes is internal iterations = 2ⁿ. Cost 10 → 14 is exactly 16384 ÷ 1024 = 16× more work per hash. The math is the point — not vague complexity.

Why it matters: "more complex" hides that the slowdown is quantifiable — you can tune it deliberately every few years as hardware gets faster.

⚠️ IMPORTANT

Common misread: "The salt is a secret the attacker needs to figure out."

Actually: the salt is public. It is stored in plain sight inside the hash string (chars 8–29 of $2b$10$<salt><hash>). If the attacker has the database, they already have every salt. The salt is not protecting you by being hidden — see "What the salt actually does" below.

Why it matters: people who think "I'll just hide the salt" go down the wrong rabbit hole. The real defence is the cost factor, not salt secrecy.

⚠️ IMPORTANT

Common misread: "What the salt actually does is make hashing harder."

Actually: the salt has two specific jobs, neither of which is being slow: (1) it makes identical passwords produce different hashes, so an attacker can't tell which users share a password; (2) it defeats precomputed rainbow tables — because the salt is random per user, no precomputed lookup table can ever match, forcing the attacker to brute-force each user's hash from scratch.

Why it matters: salt and cost are doing two different jobs. Cost = slow the per-guess work. Salt = stop the attacker from skipping the work with a precomputed table. You need both.

⚠️ IMPORTANT

Common misread: "bcrypt's slowness protects my live login API."

Actually: for a normal login it does nothing useful. The only scenario where slowness saves you is after your hash database has leaked and the attacker is brute-forcing it offline on their own hardware. Until that breach happens, you could use any hash and notice no security difference.

Why it matters: naming the actual scenario (offline crack of a stolen DB) is what turns "use bcrypt" from a magic incantation into a defensible engineering choice.

⚠️ IMPORTANT

Common misread: "Any hash works for passwords — SHA-256 is fine."

Actually: a fast hash lets an attacker test billions of stolen-hash guesses per second; bcrypt's slowness caps that at maybe a few thousand per second. Same security goal, opposite design choice from a normal hash.

Why it matters: it's the reason you don't just sha256(password) and call it done.
